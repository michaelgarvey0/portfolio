Background

"Two" many sites

Newport in Bloom and Daffodil Days are nonprofits committed to beautifying Newport, Rhode Island, with flowers. Newport in Bloom hosts city events, and Daffodil Days is a festival to celebrate the daffodils they plant in fall, which bloom every April. These two groups have significant membership overlap and wanted to combine their websites. Specifically, the Newport in Bloom site wanted to absorb Daffodil Days.

Results

  • Website costs cut in half. The hosting costs for the Newport Daffodil Days site were eliminated, and having all the information in one place simplifies their web hosting process.

  • A reorganized site map. This simplified website updates and improved user navigation. The redesign was tailored to the needs of the new Newport in Bloom webmaster and the users who would visit the site.

  • An opportunity to show off their new brand. They feel proud and happy to put their new logo on their stationery, flyers, and posters! It'll give them a new, fresh look for the coming years.

  • Full responsivity. Whatever device you're viewing the site on, its layout makes sense and adjusts to fit the screen (aka, no more zooming in on your phone).

Research

Before beginning the design process, I formulated a set of research questions to guide my investigation. These questions pertained to the current websites, the users, and the goals of the committees. The guiding questions were:

  • What are the current purposes of the sites?

  • Why do stakeholders want the sites merged and redesigned?

  • What do people think of the current sites?

  • Has anyone ever visited these sites? If so, why?

  • How do other nonprofits structure their sites?

Stakeholder interviews

Getting to know the organizations

First, I needed to understand who and what I was working with-- what do committee members want, why do they want it, and what are the sites like now? I talked to three committee members and the Newport in Bloom webmaster to figure this out.

[The site] isn’t terrible, but some things just don't make sense. I didn't even know we didn't have a page for the Decorative Baskets-- that's one of the biggest things we do all year."
Kiki, NIB Chairwoman

Several of the Newport in Bloom links went nowhere (Decorative Baskets in this screenshot), so a general dev cleanup was also requested.

The Daffodil Days site was one page, and the links at the top scrolled to specific sections of this page.

"We've always had another site for Daffy Days, but it's run by all the same people, and I don't want to have to pay for two sites."
John, "The Daffy Guy"
takeaways: stakeholder INTERVIEWS

  • Stakeholders want Daffy Days to live under Newport in Bloom. They're tired of paying for two sites, and Daffodil Days is essentially a Newport-in-Bloom-sponsored mega-event, making its placement under the NIB umbrella make sense.

  • Site navigation is a potential area of opportunity. Stakeholders believe users go to the site to learn about the events. But, they think it's hard because the current site looks outdated and is "weirdly" set up. This is something to test for while interviewing users.

  • A brand refresh is a requirement. The current logo for Newport in Bloom is a photo of an orange dahlia. This has become recognizable, and they don't want to abandon it. However, it's old-fashioned and needs some revitalization.

User interviews

Gathering user perspectives

With a clear understanding of the committees' goals, I conducted user research by interviewing 6 individuals, evenly divided between those who had participated in Newport in Bloom events (gardeners) and those who had not (non-gardeners). The aim was to gain insights into their goals and needs.

"You just feel included. [The Garden Competition] made me feel finally part of Newport after 30 years."
Len, gardener, age 52
"Honestly, I had no idea the value of [Newport in Bloom] until you told me about it, and I see it all around Newport. It brightens your day and makes me really happy to see it all."
Sue, non-gardener, age 55
"I just like to enter and see what happens, and maybe see some other people's gardens."
Shelley, gardener, age 64
takeaways: USER INTERVIEWS

  • The majority of participants are older individuals. They may not have much experience using technology. Only one participant had visited either of the websites prior to the interview.

  • People want information . Participants expected to have all their questions about anything and everything answered on the site. Who won the garden competition last year? What's the application deadline this year? What's Newport in Bloom even about?

  • The Newport in Bloom crowd is a community. Participants who had taken part in competitions reported that it was great to see everyone's garden and that they felt like it was a lot of fun, regardless of garden experience.

Peer Analysis

What are other nonprofits doing?

I also researched other nonprofit organizations as part of my investigation. I examined the websites of nonprofits in Newport, national-level organizations, and other local garden and flower-related nonprofits or festivals. This broad research helped me identify patterns across categories.

Research takeaways

  • "Donate" galore.  The primary CTA was "Donate" for almost every peer. This makes sense, as nonprofit operation relies heavily on donations.

  • Site feel intertwines with mission. For the Cherry Blossom festival, there was bright pink everywhere. In contrast, the ACLU used stark copy (short, concise sentences) and striking imagery to set a more serious tone.

  • Lots of action items. There are links to donate, get tickets, sign up for classes; any action you could possibly take is covered by a big button somewhere.

Define

After conducting research, I wanted to define the content to include as well as the framework within which to place that content. I used my research to inform these decisions, hoping to ensure that the site would be intuitive and easy to navigate.

Content and structure

Defining and organizing the information

Old site structure

The sites didn't have information about the organizations themselves. What is Newport in Bloom, and how did it come to be? How about Daffodil Days? These were questions asked by users in the secondary persona group, and they certainly couldn't be answered on the current sites.

newport in bloom: a flurry of posts

An example of a Newport in Bloom post. Most were uncategorized, and the webmaster mentioned that she didn't even like posts.

One major issue users mentioned with the current websites was that they lacked information about the organizations themselves. Questions such as "What is Newport in Bloom and how did it come to be?" or "What is Daffodil Days?" could not be answered on the current websites.

In its previous state, the Newport in Bloom website was a flurry of posts with no clear format, making it difficult to understand what content was available. The Daffodil Days website had a clearer structure, but there wasn't much content in general.

I conducted a content audit, which validated that Newport in Bloom did not have much content with general information about their events. Both websites also needed a clearer structure as users in both interview groups expressed that they would use the site in search of information, but currently, that goal would be hard to achieve.

After the audit, I sorted the content into logical groups based on feedback from user interviews and the information they wanted. These groups would become pages and sub-pages of the site. I worked with the committee members to ensure that it aligned with their goals and vision, presenting them the following sitemap:

A few notable changes I made:

  • The website was reorganized by activity. Each activity had its own dedicated page. This provided a clear and organized container for information, in contrast to the previous structure which relied on a series of posts that could be associated with multiple activities.

  • Daffodil Days is its own tab in the primary navigation. Despite technically being an "activity," the event spans an entire month and includes multiple sub-activities. Keeping it separate on the website aligns with the organization's business goals.

  • The addition of an "About" section. This is so people can learn about each organization and its members, which was expressed as a need during user interviews.

main takeaways: usability testing

  • General feedback was positive. Participants thought the design was clean and mostly easy to understand.

  • Participants didn't like the carousel on the home page . While the test validated the carousel's purpose, it didn't validate the design. Participants especially didn't like the automatic movement.

  • The events page template needed a reconfiguration . The only task that anyone failed was finding photos of last year's Garden Competition, and in each instance, they simply didn't scroll far enough down the page.

Priority fixes

Integrating user feedback

After reviewing usability testing notes, re-watching the Zoom recordings, and consulting with committee members, I identified three main issues to address, along with several additional minor improvements.

Fix 1: "Happening Now" section
previous state

An auto-rotating carousel of events and updates.

problems

  • People don't love automatically moving parts. Moving parts can detract attention and cause overwhelm.

  • Only one update was visible at a time. People like to see all their options, so the initial design violated the 'recognition rather than recall" heuristic.

  • The system was in control. The autoplay takes control from the user.

revised state

The updates are placed into vertical tabs, with each update summarized in the tab title.

How it addresses the problems

  • No moving parts. The new section is static by default.

  • All update headlines are visible. If a user is interested in one, all they have to do is click to learn more.

  • The user is in control. They can click whichever update they'd like to see.

Fix 2: Event page details
previous state

The header had action buttons in the header picture, and event information was below, separated by headings.

problems

  • People didn't know what else was on the page. Scrolling down allowed you to see photos and competition results. The header didn't tell them that.

  • The visual hierarchy was off. Participants described the headers as difficult to follow; they weren't sure where to look.

Revised state
1
Location and date information replaced the action items (which, for most events, will not exist). The header serves as a quick summary of the event.
2
Quick links for a) action items and b) section links (e.g., clicking "photos" scrolls to the photos section below)
3
General description of the event
4
"Latest news" ➞ "Updates" and boxed in a separate area
How it addresses the problem

  • The section links inform the users what information they can access from this page. This was a problem during usability testing, and some quick re-testing on that particular task proved this to be an effective solution.

  • Improved visual hierarchy. There aren't as many homogenous blocks of text-- the intention here is to drive the user's attention to the header, then to the update, and then to the rest of the copy.

Fix 3: Reconfigured sitemap
previous state

Six event pages, and Daffy Days as a primary menu item.

Sitemap v1
problems

  • Committee members wanted to include pages for NIB-sponsored events. Now, these were not accessible in the site.

  • Daffodil Days needed a restructure. Certain changes were requested by the committee. For example, the Daffodil Bulb Giveaway, while technically a "Daffy" event, took place in October, while the actual festival (called "Daffodil Days") takes places in April, and they decided this distinction was important.

takeaways: final ui

  • Designing for responsiveness was a good learning experience. I employed a grid throughout the design process, which provided a high degree of flexibility during development. Ensuring that the design was responsive was a key consideration, and using a grid made this a straightforward task.

  • Visual design is an area I aim to improve. I found the most challenging aspect of this project to be designing the logo. As I don't consider myself an artist, it highlighted the value of having a team with specialized skills working on a project, specifically someone with expertise in visual design.

  • I enjoyed collaborating with others and receiving feedback. Allowing users and stakeholders to guide the process was enjoyable as it felt like I was solving problems and helping people. It was valuable to have my biases challenged during the research and testing phases, as it reminded me that I am not the target user.
